Pebble Labs · Experiment 002

Focus Session

A short research session. We quietly watch your blink rate, gaze, and head movement through your webcam while you read or work, then ask how focused you felt a few times along the way.

Nothing is recorded or uploaded — your video never leaves this browser. Only numeric stats and your check-in answers are saved.

We also log basic interaction signals (like switching tabs) to understand distraction, and at a couple of points during the session you may see or hear a brief prompt as part of the test.

Find a well-lit spot and face your camera directly. On a phone, hold it upright — face tracking doesn't work reliably upside-down or sideways.

Reading sessions run about 5 minutes and working sessions about 10. At a couple of points you'll be asked a quick yes/no question.

You can stop at any time — nothing bad happens if you do.

Reading passage — keep reading naturally

The idea behind Pebble Labs is simple to state and hard to answer honestly: can the small, involuntary things a body does — how often it blinks, where the eyes settle, how still or restless the head is — say something real about a person's attention and fatigue, before any device is built to act on it? Most wearable products work backward from a sensor. A company builds something that can measure heart rate, or motion, or skin temperature, and only afterward asks what that measurement might be useful for. This project tries to run the argument the other way. It starts from a human problem — the way focus quietly degrades over the course of a task, long before someone notices it themselves — and asks whether any passive, low-cost signal can actually see that happening.

There is a reasonable case that it can't. Blink rate is affected by screen brightness, dryness, caffeine, and a dozen other things that have nothing to do with attention. Head posture drifts for reasons as mundane as an uncomfortable chair. Gaze stability changes with the layout of whatever someone happens to be looking at. Any one of these signals, taken alone, is a weak and noisy proxy for something as slippery as "focus." That weakness is exactly why this is being tested rather than assumed. A hypothesis that survives contact with real, messy, everyday behavior is worth far more than one that only works in a tidy lab demo.

The honest way to test a hypothesis like this is not to ask people whether they think it's plausible. It's to put a cheap, unglamorous version of it in front of real people doing real things, and see what the data actually shows. A webcam and a browser tab are not a finished product. They are an instrument — the cheapest one available — built to answer one question before any hardware gets designed: is the signal there at all? If it is, that changes what gets built next. If it isn't, that's worth knowing just as much, and probably sooner.

Reading this passage is not, itself, the point. It's simply something ordinary to do while the tracking runs quietly in the background, the same way someone might read an email, a document, or an article on an ordinary day. There's no need to read carefully, perform interest, or try to look focused for the camera. The more naturally this is read — at whatever pace, with whatever lapses in attention actually happen — the more useful the resulting data is. A session where attention wanders exactly as it normally would is a better session than one where someone is trying hard to look engaged.

It also helps to be clear about what this kind of study cannot claim. A few dozen short sessions will not prove that any signal predicts attention across all people, all devices, and all kinds of work. What they can do is show whether the effect is large enough to be worth chasing at all, which patterns repeat from person to person, and which ones vanish the moment conditions change. Small, honest evidence like that is the right foundation for a bigger study. Overstating it would be the fastest way to lose the trust that makes the next, harder experiment possible.

What happens after this test matters as much as the test itself. If the signals turn out to carry real information, the next step is not a bigger website or a better dashboard. It is a harder question: can that information be turned into something useful at the moment it matters, such as a quiet nudge to rest your eyes, shift your posture, or step away before fatigue quietly erodes the next hour of work? That only makes sense if the underlying signal is trustworthy first, which is why this small, plain experiment comes before everything else.

If the mind drifts partway through this — to a notification, a sound in the next room, a stray thought about something else entirely — that's not a failure of the test. It's the exact thing being measured. The goal from here is just to keep reading, at a natural pace, until the session ends on its own.
